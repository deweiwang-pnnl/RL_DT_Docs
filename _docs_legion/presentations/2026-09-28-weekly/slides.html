The lift blocker

It was our gripper setup — four defects, each measured

  • Pad joint driven to 0 — copied from the URDF; in the USD it closes the four-bar loop and must follow +q. Pads bent into a V (12° at contact).
  • Grasp height — pads travel 18 mm toward the table while closing; they hit the table.
  • Closing axis — fingers closed across the 128 mm side, not the 85 mm side.
  • Contact physics — pads sank through the plate under a two-sided squeeze; Isaac Lab's own 2F-140 settings fixed it.

Also: robot gravity off for now (the arm stalled 0.3–0.4 m short without compensation) — the realistic arm came later (slide 12).

7
Centred stacking

Three findings that made centring learnable

1 · Tell the policy which way to turn

Twist between the plates as an observation (sin 2Δ, cos 2Δ — either end is fine). Before, it had to decode it from two quaternions and the bottom plate's 49° mesh offset.

2 · Raise the bar in steps

Twist tolerance 45° → 30° → 20° → 10° → 5° → 3°, centring 15 → 10 → 5 mm. At 10° straight away nothing was ever rewarded.

3 · Make letting go worth it

Success ends the episode but the value is bootstrapped, so the one-off success bonus is the only gain from releasing. Rewards are scaled by the 1/30 s step: a bonus of 50 paid 1.7 — below the critic's noise. 600 → C1a 1.6 % → 67 %.

Three plates — how

One policy failed; two skills and two curricula worked

  • One policy for both placements forgot the first placement (the weights were overwritten — swap test) and finally learned not to grasp at all.
  • Two skills: C3 places the first plate; a second policy, trained from it, places the third.
  • Rising stack: trained at full height directly, the second skill got 0.8 %. A kinematic middle plate rising 0 → 26 mm: 70–92 % at each step, 91 % at full height.
  • Hand-off start states: its first move used to drag the plate just placed (> 5 mm in 61 % of cases within 0.17 s). Training from 2000 recorded hand-offs fixed it.
  • Loose bottom plate: mass curriculum 20 kg → 50 g, plus a penalty for pushing it.

Realistic arm

Gravity on, compensated like a real UR controller — no retraining needed

A real UR arm cancels gravity in its joint controller. Our simulated joints are PD springs (400 / 80), so we add the compensation torque G(q) from PhysX's inverse dynamics every physics step.

Other libraries

Same task, from scratch, same budget — one seed each

  • Reach: rsl_rl, skrl, rl_games PPO all 100 % in 11–14 min.
  • Align from scratch: only skrl PPO (98.4 %).
  • Why skrl beats rsl_rl here is open: a learning-rate explanation was tested and rejected. Next test: value normalisation.

Reading the numbers

Why we report deterministic evaluation, not the training curve

The blue line is what TensorBoard shows: success with exploration noise. With a binary gripper, noise opens the grip at random, so it often falls within a stage while the real (deterministic) success — orange dots — rises.

Every gate and every number in this deck is the deterministic one, 128 episodes, from a separate evaluation script.

14
Limits

What these numbers do not show yet

  • One seed per training run. No variance yet — the largest gap in the evidence.
  • Final setting fails 45 % of the time: 36 % time-outs (second placement unfinished or middle plate pushed out of tolerance), 9 % drops.
  • Two policies, switched by the environment's phase — not one policy that decides when the first plate is done.
  • Privileged state: the policy sees exact plate poses from the simulator; no camera yet.
  • Robot base locked; plates start in fixed pick areas; plate mass / friction not randomised.
  • Realistic arm = gravity + compensation only; no joint friction, latency or torque limits beyond the sim's.
